\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\subsubsection{Larval zebrafish as an animal system for neuroscience}
The zebrafish (\textit{Danio rerio}), a small freshwater fish from india, is a commonly used animal system in mechanistic neuroscience. In its larval stage, days after hatching, its small size, transparency, and the genetic toolkit, allows for minimally invasive functional imaging of the whole brain, in a living vertebrate (\autoref{fig:introduction}B).\\
One facet of neuroscience the larval zebrafish is used in is the analysis of decision making. Exposing the fish to visual stimuli and observing the swim directions they choose to take, allows for high-throughput decision making experiments.\\
Through the creation of mathematical models, which reproduce the behavioral responses of these experiments, and finding neural correlates of the parts of these models in the brain seeing these same stimuli, it is possible to propose functional cell types and neural circuits for decision making. The task studied range from fundamental orientation in the environment based on visual cues \cite{bahlNeuralCircuitsEvidence2020, slangewalVisuomotorDecisionmakingMultifeature2026, boulanger-weillCorrelativeLightElectron2025}, to more specialized behaviors such as distinguishing between predator and prey \cite{barkerSensorimotorDecisionMaking2015}, or finding conspecifics for social interactions \cite{kappelVisualRecognitionSocial2022}.  

\subsubsection{The optomotor response}
\label{sec:omr}
One of these behavioral paradigms is the optomotor response (OMR). It describes the tendency of the larval zebrafish to follow the overall direction of visual motion \cite{orgerBehavioralScreeningAssays2004b, naumannWholeBrainDataFunctional2016}. Bahl and Engert \cite{bahlNeuralCircuitsEvidence2020} established a neural circuit, based on a leaky integrator model. This circuit was further investigated by Boulanger-Weill and collaborators \cite{boulanger-weillCorrelativeLightElectron2025},
using electron microscopy, axon-tracing, and HCR to validate and update the connections and neurotransmitters of the neurons of the circuit. Showing that the real-life implementation of the neural circuit is more complex then proposed, as they found multiple midline-crossing cell types, and mixed populations of excitatory and inhibitory neuron within one functional cell type.\\
The proposed circuit consists of three functional cell types: \textit{Motion Integrator} (MI) cells, which rises towards a plateau  while there is motion in the preferred direction of the neuron. MIs can either project ipsilateral or contralateral  (iMI/cMI). The cMIs are almost exclusively inhibitory, as they express \textit{glutamate decarboxylase 1b} (\textit{gad1b}), an enzyme needed for the production of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, repressing the contralateral part of the circuit, while there is visual motion in ipsilateral direction.\\
Meanwhile, the iMI population is mixed in their neurotransmitter. The excitatory subset, which expresses the gene \textit{vglut2a}, a marker for neurons releasing \textit{glutamate}, an excitatory transmitter, builds up the signal strength through recursive connections, then forwards the impulse further downstream in the circuit, while the inhibitory, \textit{gad1b$^+$}, ipsilateral MIs remove the inhibition of MIs from the second functional cell type:
\textit{Motion Onset} (MON) cells. MONs reach their peak response signal earlier then the other cell types, and then fall of as the MIs reach their peak activity. They serve sort of as a "handbrake" for the whole circuit, allowing the animal to accumulate more information before the circuit generates an output (\autoref{fig:introduction}A, C).\\
The final cell type are the \textit{Slow Motion Integrator} (SMI) cells. As their name suggest, SMIs' response slowly rises as the stimulus goes on. They are proposed to be the output nodes of the circuit, connecting to myelinated neurons, which dictate motor output.\\
Overall, the circuit works as follows: If the larva experiences visual motion to e.g. the left side of itself, the iMI and MON cells in the left anterior hindbrain get activated by motion processing cells from the midbrain. The MONs surpress activity of motion integrating cells until there is a strong enough signal in the inhibitory subset of iMIs. They cause inhibition of MON neurons, in turn releasing cMIs to be able to inhibit all part of the circuit on the contralateral hemisphere. In addition to this SMIs are activates by iMIs, forwarding the signal to myelinated neurons, which initiate swimming to the left (\autoref{fig:introduction}C).\\

\subsubsection{Phototaxis}
\label{sec:phototaxis}
Another well studied visually driven behavior is phototaxis. In larval zebrafish, phototaxis describes the tendency of larvae to swim towards brighter illuminated areas \cite{orgerBehavioralScreeningAssays2004b, liZebrafishMutantsBehavioral2001, burgessDistinctRetinalPathways2010}.\\
This response can also be observed if a "fish-centered" stimulus, meaning the visual field of the fish is split into a bright and dark half at the midline of the larva, is presented. Larvae will predominantly swim towards the bright side \cite{capelleBehavioralAlgorithmsOntogenetic2026}.\\
Slangewal et al. proposed a neural mechanism for this \cite{slangewalVisuomotorDecisionmakingMultifeature2026}. They studied the combination of the above described "split-view" luminance cues with random-dot-motion (RDM) stimuli, in which flickering dots are moving on horizontally to the fish.\\
The study revealed multiple functional cell types, reacting to luminance: \textit{Luminance Integrators} (LI), which similar to MIs build up their signal with time when the larva is exposed to bright light, \textit{Bright} and \textit{Dark}, as well as general \textit{Change} neurons, which all react to changes in luminance on the contralateral eye. Furthermore, \textit{Motion} cells, showing identical responses to MIs, and \textit{Multifeature} cells, combining motion and luminance input, were described (\autoref{fig:introduction}D, E).\\
In the proposed circuit, motion and luminance integration, as well as the change in luminance, happen in parallel. While motion is processed on the ipsilateral hindbrain hemisphere, luminance responsive cells are found in the optic tectum, contralateral to the eye which receives the stimulus. The two path converge in the \textit{Multifeature} cells, which then initiate further neurons, which elicits a swimming bout.\\

\subsubsection{Development of larval zebrafish behaviors}

The stereotypical behavior of the OMR can first be observed at 4dpf \cite{orgerBehavioralScreeningAssays2004b}. One of the questions explored in this work is whether this is due to lack of processing of the visual information in the brain, or general lack of locomotor ability. This question is not trivial, as other behaviors such as phototaxis and the optokinetic response are reported to be present as early as 3dpf \cite{liZebrafishMutantsBehavioral2001}. Therefore, comparing the neural activity between larvae of different ages is crucial to understand the development of these neural circuits.

\subsection{Aims of this work}
The main motivation when starting this work, was to further investigate and profile the functional cell types involved in decision-making circuits. Previously there had been multiple different approaches like photoactivated axon tracing, neurotransmitter classification, or electron microscopy imaging \cite{boulanger-weillCorrelativeLightElectron2025, slangewalVisuomotorDecisionmakingMultifeature2026}.\\
Here, two other approaches of investigation were as followed; the development of the functional cell types and thereby the neural circuit, and also the gene expression in the neurons, using a publicly available dataset.\\
This will lead to an even deeper understanding of the cells responsible for vision-based decision making. The developmental aspect showing when these neuron are first detectable, and also how their response develops, while the investigation of gene expression could serve as a first step towards, functional cell type specific, genetic markers.\\


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Two-photon functional imaging}

\subsubsection{Used fish lines and handling}
\label{sec:fishlines}
Transgenic fish containing either a cytosolic \textit{Tg(elavl3: GCaMP8s)} or nuclear \textit{Tg(elavl3: H2B-GCaMP8s)} localized version of the calcium-modulated green fluorophore GCaMP were used. The lines had a \textit{mitfa} negative background, meaning double negative larvae produce no pigments and are transparent. These fish were screened for GCaMP expression, embedded in a drop of 2\% agarose  (Ultra Pure Low Melting Point Agarose, 16520-100, Invitrogen) in E3 fish water (5mM NaCl, 0.18mM KCl, 0.33mM CaCl$_2$, 0.4mM MgCl$_2$, buffered with NaHCO$_3$ to pH 7.4), in the center of a petri dish, and oriented with a small pipette tip. The solidified agarose was then covered with E3 water and left for 30min to 1h at 20$^\circ$C in the dark microscopy room, allowing the agarose to completely solidify and the fish to acclimatize.\\

\subsubsection{Microscope setup}
The functional imaging was preformed with a two-photon microscope custom-build by Katja Slangewal, Moritz Fuchsloch, Max Capelle, and Armin Bahl.\\
In this setup, a femtosecond-pulsed, tunable, DeepSee MaiTai Ti:Sapphire laser (SpectraPhysics) is used to achieve the high temporal and spatial photon density needed for two-photon excitation. The pulsing of the excitation laser has the added benefit of reducing fluorophore bleaching and avoids heat build-up inside the specimen.\\
In addition to the pulsing, the power of the laser was reduced to 15-20$mW$ average power using a $\lambda/2$ plate  (Thorlabs, AHWP05M-980) and Glan-Thompson prism (Thorlabs, GL5-A), which reflects parts of the laser beam into a beam dump (Thorlabs, BT610/M).\\
Fast moving mirrors, galvanometers (Cambridge Technology), at the top of the microscope, raster the excitation beam across a definable area in  x- and y-direction, while a 25x NA 1.1 objective (CFI75 Apo 25XC W 1300, Nikon) focuses it on a specific z-plane and collects the emitted light.\\
The fluorescence signal is reflected by a dichroic mirror, passes through a set of filters, and is then detected by a photomultiplier tube (PMT).\\
A detailed description of the microscope can be found here \cite{slangewalMultifeatureVisualProcessing}.\\ % Katja PHD thesis

\subsubsection{Stimulus configuration}
\label{sec:stimuli}
Using a small projector (P300 Neo Pico Projector, Aaxa Technologies) and a mirror, the visual stimuli were projected onto a diffusive paper below the fish. The stimuli were presented in red, as to not interfere with the green fluorescence signal.\\
For the motion and luminance stimuli, each trial started with a 5s \textit{baseline} stimulus of randomly appearing and disappearing, stationary, bright dots on a \textit{"gray"} (in practice medium intensity red) background, followed by 25s of stimulus presentation, and finally, 10s of the same \textit{baseline} stimulus (\autoref{fig:overview}A).\\
During the 25s of stimulus presentation the scene either switched to a \textit{luminance split-view}, with the background on one side of the midline of the fish, turning brighter and the other darker, while the dots stayed stationary and flickering, or a RDM stimulus. Both of the stimuli types were presented in a \textit{left} or \textit{right} configuration. Stimulus type and direction would appear in random order but equally often.\\
To reproduce the WARP datasets stimuli, which are quite short, each stimulus started with 3s of gray, then for 3s, either "sine-gratings" (smooth edged bars), moving forward, backward, left, or right, a "loom" (an expanding black circle), from the left or right side were shown, or the whole arena turned bright. After this a gray background was shown for 9s (\autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}B).\\
During data processing, the stimuli were regrouped based on the position of the cell aligned to the reference atlas, meaning the \textit{luminance split-view} stimulus with the bright side on the left, would be classified as \textit{luminance ipsi}, if the cell was in the left, or \textit{luminance contra}, if the cell was in the right hemisphere of the brain. The same was done for the RDM stimulus, based on the direction of movement of the dots.\\

\subsubsection{Imaging}
The embedded larvae were placed inside of the microscope and the objective immersed into the water. Stimulus projection and stage position were adjusted to have the midline of the fish correctly positioned. Then the fluorescence imaging was set up to show a square region of the brain, which focused on the anterior hindbrain and optic tectum, at a resolution of 0.5 x 0.5$\mu m$ per pixel, and with a image acquisition rate of 1 to 2$Hz$.\\
Before any image acquisition, forward moving bars were shown to the fish for around 30min. This causes the fish to stop moving, which reduces motion artifacts during the imaging.\\
For each fish a overview stack with a z resolution of 2$\mu m$, spanning a large section of the brain was acquired. This was later used to register the imaging data to the reference brain.
After that, between 3 and 8 (in most fish 5 or 6) different functional z-planes were imaged per fish. The z-planes were spaced about 10 to 15$\mu m$ apart from each other to ensure that different cells would be imaged. At every z-plane the stimuli were presented for 8 to 20 trials each.\\
Some fish were imaged overnight for 12 to 15h in repeats, meaning the same 5 or 6 z-planes were imaged for 4 or 5 times. So that the same cells were imaged multiple times as the larva was aging. For these experiments the \textit{age} value, of the two final repeats was set to the next day.\\

\subsubsection{Imaging data preprocessing}
\label{sec:image preproc}
To process to two-photon imaging data, \textit{Full2PPreProcessing}, a python 3.12 pipeline created by Katja Slangewal was used \cite{slangewalMultifeatureVisualProcessing}.\\
It applies motion correction using \textit{CaImAn}, aligns the image stack to the different stimuli, binning it to a certain $\Delta t$, creates a time-averaged stack, and performs \textit{cellpose} segmentation \cite{pachitariuCellposeSAMSuperhumanGeneralization2025} on the average image of each plane, creating individual cell segments for which the average pixel value across the segment is taken for each point in time (fluorescence trace) (\autoref{fig:overview}B). As well as, using \textit{ANTs} to anatomically register the time-averaged stack to a reference brain volume.\\
The above mentioned overview stack were registered to the \textit{ZBrain} reference brain \cite{randlettWholebrainActivityMapping2015} (\autoref{fig:registration}), while the functional planes were first registered to the specific overview stack of the fish, after this, the already acquired transformation from the overview stack to the reference brain was then applied to the functional stack, registering it to the reference brain. This allowed each segmented cell to be assigned to a brain region of the \textit{ZBrain} atlas as well as its brain hemisphere.\\
\textit{CaImAn} \cite{giovannucciCaImAnOpenSource2019} motion-correction, \textit{cellpose} segmentation, and \textit{ANTs} registration used the default parameters of \textit{Full2PPreProcessing}, after the preprocessing, the results were visually inspected to check if most visible cells were segmented and if the overlay of the transformed image stack and reference looked plausible. The the segmentation or registration parameters were adjusted and rerun until results were deemed satisfactory.\\
As the registration of the cytosolic GCaMP fish to the \textit{ZBrain} reference proved difficult, these overview stacks were first registered to the \textit{mapzebrain} reference brain \cite{kunstCellularResolutionAtlasLarval2019, MapZebrain}, after which a bridge transform from \textit{mapzebrain} to \textit{ZBrain} could be used to bring them in the same reference system as the other samples.\\
The final output of the \textit{Full2PPreProcessing} pipline is an HDF5 file, from which a pandas dataframe, containing each fluorescence trace indexed by \textit{segment ID}, \textit{stimulus}, and \textit{trial}, could be created in python. In this step the cell segments generated by \textit{cellpose} where filtered based on their $area$ and $roundness\ (R)$.\\
A segment $i$ is valid if;
\[
Q_{1}(A) \cdot 1.1 \leq A_i \leq 2 \cdot median(A) 
\]
where $A=\{A_0,...,A_N\}$ are the areas of all segments and and $Q_1(A)$ is the first percentile of areas.\\
And;
\[
R_i \geq 0.6\ with\ R_i= \frac{4 \pi A_i}{P_i}
\]
where $P_i$ is the perimeter of segment $i$.\\
Area and perimeter where calculated using the \textit{contourArea} and \textit{arcLength} functions of the \textit{cv2} python library.\\
This removed segmentation artefacts, ensuring only correctly segmented cells were processed going forward.\\
Segments not assigned to any region in the reference brain were also removed, as they were also likely to be artifacts.

\subsection{Data processing and analysis}

\subsubsection{Active cell detection}
\label{sec:activity detection}
For each segmented cell and stimulus 8 to 20 trials were recorded. In a first step, the fluorescence traces of each trial were normalized by:
\[
    \frac{\Delta F_{i,t}}{F_0} = \frac{F_{i,t} - F_0}{F_0}\ (in\ text\ written\ \Delta F/F_0)
\]
where $F_{i,t}$ is the average pixel value of the pixels of one cell segment for trial $i$ at time point $t$, and $F_0$ is the baseline fluorescence for this cell and stimulus, defined by the median across a trials and time points in the pre-stimulus period from 0 to 5s \cite{HowComputeDF}.\\
Then cells were deemed active if;
\[
max(| \frac{\Delta F_{stim}}{F_0}|) > 0.3\ and\
max(|F_{stim} - F_0|) > 6 \sigma
\]
where $\sigma = StandardDeviation(F_{pre stim} - F_0)$ across all trials and $F_{stim}$ being the fluorescence of all time points after 5s (start of the stimulus), was both true for at least $30\%$ of trials of one stimulus.\\

\subsubsection{Trial consolidation using Dynamic-Time-Warping}
\label{sec:dtw trials}
To summarize these individual traces of active cells into one representative trace, the \textit{TimeSeriesKMeans} class from the python library \textit{tslearn} \cite{tavenardTslearnMachineLearning2020} was used. \textit{Dynamic-Time-Warping (DTW)} is a similarity measure of two time series, it accounts for phase shifts in the signals, which means that fast cell responses, which might not happen at the exact same time, will still be grouped together. For the trial processing, the \textit{Sakoe-Chiba} constraint was set to allow only shifts of 4 time steps (2s) in each direction \cite{sakoeDynamicProgrammingAlgorithm1978b}.\\
This method was chosen, over an averaging method like the arithmetic mean or median, because of the observation % add sources
that neurons will not respond every single time a stimulus is presented. The \textit{DTW} approach avoids flattening the response of cells which respond characteristically but unreliably, through averaging and works on the underlying assumption that there are two groups of trials \textit{no response} and \textit{response}. Therefore, \textit{TimeSeriesKMeans} is set to classify the trials into two clusters. From these classifications the two centroids time series, also called \textit{barycenters} are produced, which are representative "average" traces of the two clusters \cite{petitjeanGlobalAveragingMethod2011}. These centroids are compared using the following score:
\[
score_x = Q_{95}(C_x) - Q_{5}(C_x) - median(|x_t-x_{t+1}|\ for \ t\ \epsilon\ \{0,...,T-1\})
\]
where $C_x$ is the centroid trace $C_x = \{x_0,..., x_T\}$ and $Q_q$ is the $q$-th percentile of that centroid trace. This scoring methods favors signals with high signal range but also low variability across the signal.\\
After picking the cluster with the highest scoring centroid, the \textit{DTW} distances ($D=\{d_0,...,d_N\}$) between each of the N trials and the cluster centroid was calculated and converted into weights ($W=\{w_0,..., w_N\}$) using an exponential kernel.
\[
W = exp(-\frac{D}{median(D)})
\]
The final, trial adjusted, $\Delta F/F_0$ trace was then produced by adding the individual trials according to their weights, meaning every trial contributed to the final fluorescence trace.\\
In addition, this method produced an additional metric \textit{reliability} for each cell and stimulus, which is the fraction of trials that were classified in the top scoring centroid trace. In the further analysis only cells with \textit{reliability} $\geq 0.3$ in a least half of the stimuli were used.\\
After this the trace data for each fish was combined to a multi-index dataframe with the index \textit{(fish ID, age, brain region, repeat, z-plane, cell ID, stimulus)}.\\

\subsubsection{Functional cell type classification}
The stereotypical responses of the functional cell types described above (\hyperref[sec:omr]{1.1.2}, \hyperref[sec:phototaxis]{1.1.3}), had been created by either modeling cell types based on behavior experiments (luminance cell types), or by clustering and averaging the responses of cells in the anterior hindbrain (motion cell types).\\
These regressors were used to calculate a correlation coefficient ($\rho$) for each cell and functional cell type, using the \textit{pearsonr} function of the \textit{scipy.stats} library \cite{virtanenSciPy10Fundamental2020}. For motion cell types only the RDM stimulus in ipsilateral direction was used, while for luminance cell types all four stimuli were used to calculate $\rho$.\\
Cells with $\rho \geq 0.1$ to a cell type and difference to the next best cell type of 0.05 or greater were assigned this cell type if the \textit{Benjamini-Hochberg} \cite{benjaminiControllingFalseDiscovery1995} corrected false discovery rate of \textit{pearsonr} was less then or equal to 0.05.\\

\subsection{Gene expression analysis using the WARP dataset}
\subsubsection{Used data and experimental setup}
The WARP dataset is a publicly available dataset combining fictive behavior, functional calcium imaging, and gene expression data for 41 genes on a single cell scale. It contains, among other data, neuronal activity of responses to 8 visual stimuli (\autoref{fig:warpfig}).\\
To extract gene expression information about the functional cell types of interest, four 5dpf old fish where imaged both with the afore mentioned RDM and split-view luminance stimuli and with a stimuli replicating the WARP stimulus.\\
The stimuli were: moving bars in four directions, whole field bright luminance and an expanding dark circle on either side of the fish (loom stimulus) (\hyperref[sec:stimuli]{see 2.1.3}, \autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}B). As in the WARP experiments the stimulus duration were presented for a short time (3s) and recorded a faster image acquisition rate of 2$Hz$. The dark stimulus of the WARP dataset was not replicated, because it had even fewer data points then the other stimuli.\\

\subsubsection{Processing of the WARP dataset}
\label{sec:warp proc}
The dataset was downloaded from the \href{https://figshare.com/s/d1d19b105c4f74865c32}{repository} provided in the publication. Trial averaged and normalized traces were split into the different stimuli according to the provided timings. Cells that had traces containing only \textit{NAN} or 0 were removed. Then cells were deemed active if they a higher or equal absolute peak response then the median absolute peak response of all cells, in a least two stimuli.\\
Then all cells expressing \textit{cx43} were removed as they are likely glia cells.\\
The remaining cells where assigned to the left hemisphere if their x-coordinate was less then 281, and to the right if it was higher then 289. These boundaries were based on the \textit{mapzebrain} reference brain, to which the cells of the WARP dataset are registered to.
All cells not assigned to one hemisphere were removed. After this, the left/right directional stimuli were renamed to \textit{ipsi}/\textit{contra } based on the hemisphere the cell was on.\\

\subsubsection{Identification of motion integrator cells in the WARP dataset}
\label{sec:warp mi}
As there were two different sets stimuli used to make the connection between the functional cell types described above (\hyperref[sec:omr]{1.1.2}, \hyperref[sec:phototaxis]{1.1.3}) with the WARP dataset, and due to the processing of the imaging data, the same cell segments had to be found in both functional imaging stacks to be able to compare the cells responses to all stimuli.\\
To achieve this the distances between xy-centroids of all cell segments after registration to the overview stack (see \hyperref[sec:image preproc]{2.1.5}) were calculated using \textit{scipy.spatial.cKDTree} class \cite{maneewongvatanaAnalysisApproximateNearest1999}.\\
From this distance matrix, for each cell segment, the closest segments within a $2\mu m$ radius, were selected and checked if their shape was similar based on area and perimeter (compare \hyperref[sec:image preproc]{2.1.5}). If these parameters were at least 80\% similar, they were noted as a match.\\     
MI cells were then identified using the regressor trace (\autoref{fig:overview}D, \autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}A). Cells were classified as a MI if they had a \textit{pearson} $\rho$ greater or equal then 0.7 for the MI regressor and at least 0.1 difference to the next best cell type.\\
Then the average trace of the recreate WARP stimuli for these cells were created using \textit{tslearn's softdtw\_barycenters} \cite{tavenardTslearnMachineLearning2020, cuturiSoftDTWDifferentiableLoss2017}. For this the moving bars in all four directions as well as the loom stimuli, but not the whole field luminance stimulus, were used, as there was little response of the MI cells to this stimulus. (\autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}C, D)\\
Using these average responses to the stimuli, cells from the WARP dataset were selected based if their Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.5 or higher.\\
From these correlated cells, cells assigned to the \textit{rhombencephalon} region of the reference brain and within the bounds of $185 < x < 385,\ 460 < y < 630$, were selected as potential MI cells.\\

\subsubsection{Detection of differentially expressed genes}
To find differentially expressed genes, the HCR spot count gene expression data of the WARP dataset was compared between the selected potential MI cells and all cells in the \textit{rhombencephalon} and within the above described boundaries.\\
Significance between the distribution of gene counts was detected by a two-sided Mann-Whitney U-test, as well as comparing Wasserstein distances of the MI cell subset and 10,000 randomly selected same size cell subsets to the all hindbrain cells in the coordinate boundaries. Genes were significantly differentially expressed if the Benjamini-Hochberg corrected false discovery rate was below 5\% for both procedures and if the difference between the Wasserstein distance of the MI cell subset and the median of the 10,000 randomly selected cell subsets' Wasserstein distances was larger then 1.\\
The Wasserstein distance between the MI cell subset and the full cell set was used as the effect size of the change in gene expression. The direction of the change was set by comparing the median non zero gene counts (\autoref{fig:volcano}).\\

\subsubsection{Imaging set-up}
Our two-photon-microscope setup allows us to project a visual stimulus from underneath alive larvae fixed with agarose, while % add figure of set up
simultaneously acquiring single-cell-resolution fluorescence brain images at a rate of  1 to 2$Hz$. The pan-neuronally expressed nuclear calcium indicator GCaMP (\hyperref[sec:fishlines]{see 2.1.1}) changes its fluorescence based on the calcium concentration in the cell, which serves as an indirect read-out of the activity of the neuron.\\

For fish aged  3, 4, and 5dpf, multiple different z-planes were imaged, focusing on the anterior hindbrain, the optic tectum and parts of the pretectum, as the cells of interest described above are found in these regions (see \hyperref[sec:omr]{1.1.2}, \hyperref[sec:phototaxis]{1.1.3}).\\
On each of the z-planes the stimuli were presented in random order for 8 to 20 times. Some fish were imaged overnight, recording cell activity from the same z planes about every  3 hours.\\
In total 4 larva were imaged at 3dpf, 4 overnight from 3 to 4dpf, 1 at 4dpf, 2 from 4 to 5dpf, and 7 at 5dpf.\\

\subsubsection{Imaging Data Processing}
Using the two-photon pre-processing pipeline, the fluorescence image stacks were motion-corrected, aligned to the stimulus presentations, the outlines of single cells segmented, and the average stacks registered to the reference brain ZBrain. This yielded a fluorescence time series for each segmented cell area, stimulus and trial, as well as coordinates and assigned region in the reference brain for a total of 316,666 cells.\\
After this, active cells were selected based on their $\Delta F/F_{0}$ fluorescence (\hyperref[sec:activity detection]{see 2.2.1}) and trials were averaged using \textit{DTW} (\hyperref[sec:dtw trials]{see 2.2.2}), resulting in a total of 109,886 active cells.\\
Comparing the fraction of active cells, selected by the analysis above, already shows a difference between 3, 4 and 5dpf old fish. While the total number of neurons naturally increased with age as the brain is growing, the number of of active cell increases faster then the total number, meaning a higher fraction of active cells at the later ages (\autoref{tbl:cell counts}, \autoref{fig:active fraction}).\\
In the majority of annotated reference brain regions (47 regions that contained at least 20 segmented cells), the fraction of active cells increased with age (in 36 regions of 3 to 4dpf, 41 from 3 to 5dpf, and 40 in 4 to 5dpf).\\

\subsection{Age dependent analysis of luminance and motion responses}

\subsubsection{Regressor classification of known functional cell types}
As described above, the functional cell types that respond most prominently, and are thought to be responsible for the larva's behavior, were already investigated and described previously. Therefore, it was possible to use ideal responses of these cell types as regressors to classify cells.\\ % do figure showing the regressor traces
Two subsets of calcium response regressors were used to classify neurons from larvae of 3, 4 and 5dpf.
The first set of regressors contained the characteristic responses of hindbrain OMR cells (\textit{Motion Integrator} (MI), \textit{Motion Onset} (MON), and \textit{Slow Motion Integrator} (SMI) ) to motion in their preferred direction (equal to the hemisphere they are on). % refer to Armin and Flo for the regressors
The second set was used to detect cells that respond to the changes in luminance during the split-view stimulus, while not having a response to the motion stimuli (\textit{Bright}, \textit{Dark}, \textit{Change}, and \textit{Luminance Integrator} (LI) ).\\ % refere to Katja for regressors
Cells were assigned a functional cell type based on the regressors, if they had a significant correlation coefficient ($\rho$) of 0.1 or above, at least 0.05 higher then the next highest correlating cell type.\\
For the OMR regressor set only cells assigned to the \textit{Rhombencephalon} region of the  reference brain atlas were used, as the cell types of interest are proposed to be located there, while luminance sensitive could be located in the \textit{Diencephalon}, \textit{Mesencephalon}, or \textit{Rhombencephalon}.\\
This cell type classification revealed that, while characteristic luminance sensitive cell types could already be identified at similar frequency and with similar or higher average $\rho$ at the age of 3dpf, compared to 4 and 5dpf, except for LI cells, where $\rho$ was significantly lower in 3dpf then in 4 and 5dpf larva. On the other hand, all three cell types believed to be responsible for OMR were less frequently detected in the hindbrain of 3dpf fish, and the correlation coefficients were significantly lower then in larvae ages 4 or 5dpf and $\rho$ was significantly higher in 5 then in 4dpf fish. Showing a continuous increase in OMR cell activity during aging from 3 to 5dpf. (\autoref{fig:boxplots corr}, \autoref{fig:corr traces}).\\
While the difference in between classified cells from 3 and 5dpf old fish are significant in a Mann-Whitney U-test for \textit{Bright} and \textit{Change} cell types, the actual differences in median $\rho$ are small (0.25 to 0.28 in \textit{Bright}, and 0.36 to 0.35 in
\textit{Change} cells). In \textit{Dark} cells there is no change in $\rho$ across ages but for the \textit{Luminance Integrator} cell type correlation is significantly lower at 3dpf (median 0.39) then at 4 and 5dpf (0.44 and 0.45) (\autoref{fig:boxplots corr}A).\\
In the hindbrain OMR cell types the progression of median $\rho$ with age is the same for all three cell types, rising from 0.47, 0.47, 0.49 for MI, MON, and SMI cells at 3dpf to 0.6, 0.5, 0.57 at 4dpf and 0.73, 0.6, 0.64 at 5dpf (\autoref{fig:boxplots corr}B).\\
Generally, the $\rho$ are expected to be lower for the luminance cell types as correlation with the regressor is performed across all four stimuli, limiting the selected cells to pure luminance responsive cells, while the OMR celltype classification only considers the ipsilateral RDM stimulus, meaning selected cells might have different responses to the other stimuli. \\
The fraction of classified cells (difference of 0.05 or greater between $\rho$ for cell types) is similar across ages for most functional cell types except for MI and SMI cells, where a higher fraction of cells are detected at 5dpf then at 3dpf (\autoref{fig:corr traces}).\\

\subsubsection{Age differences in regional activity}
As described above, the fraction of active cells rises with age in most annotated regions of the brain. Comparing the active cell fractions at the different ages in hindbrain regions, showed that some of the highest changes in the fraction of active cells are in the regions in which the OMR cells are detected, namely Rhombencephalon Gad1b clusters 1, 2, and 17 (\autoref{fig:changing regions}). \\ % Bahl and Engert
Furthermore, selecting \textit{Rhombencephalon} regions which showed a change in the fraction of activity higher then the median, and looking at the median response to the RDM stimulus (\autoref{fig:region activity}A), of the cells located in these regions for each age, revealed that the median trace of 4 and 5dpf fish had a clear response to the stimulus in most of hindbrain regions with the highest change in the fraction of active cells, while for the 3dpf median trace no clear coordinated response was detectable.\\
In addition to this, comparing the regressor OMR cell type classification between ages, for each of the top changing hindbrain regions, showed that there are significant differences in $\rho$  (\autoref{fig:region activity}B).\\
For example the median $\rho$ of cells 3dpf in the Gad1b cluster 1 are 0.47 for MI, 0.53 for MON, and 0.56 for SMI cells, while the median $\rho$ is 0.86, 0.7, 0.82 for those cell types of 5dpf fish in this region. Gad1b cluster 2 shows similar values, which go from 0.42, 0.47, and 0.48 at 3dpf to 0.78, 0.72, 0.79 at 5dpf. In other regions, not all median cell type correlations change significantly, in Gad1b cluster 17 median $\rho$ changes between 3 and 5dpf, from 0.45 and 0.56 to 0.81 and 0.86 in MI and SMI cells while staying relatively unchanged for MON cells (0.54 to 0.62). Two other interesting regions are Gad1b cluster 16 and Gad1b Stripe 1, which show high increases in median $\rho$ for the SMI cell type (0.7, 0.74 at 5dpf and 0.46, 0.51 at 3dpf).\\

\subsubsection{Functional clustering of cells}
Because the regressors were developed from experiments with fish aged 5dpf, there might be a bias towards the cell types or, more generally, the shape of calcium traces of 5dpf neurons. To investigate this clustering, using HDBSCAN was performed on the stimulus responses of the cells. The variables were the Pearson correlations between the different stimulus responses of one cell, as well a trace based metrics; peak $\Delta F/F_{0}$ value, time to 90th percentile, to after 90th percentile was reached to 70th percentile, and \textit{reliability} (see above).\\
HDBSCAN is sensitive to its initialization parameters. To account for this, a set of 1080 configurations was tested for the active cells of the three ages, once for \textit{Diencephalon}, \textit{Mesencephalon}, and \textit{Rhombencephalon} cells with the two directions of split-view luminance and once for\textit{Rhombencephalon} cells with the RDM stimuli. For each parameter the number of non-noise cluster which passed checks based on activity and volatility (see Methods . . .) was counted. This served as an unbiased measure of how stereotypical the cells responses are and if any functional cell types can be classified.\\

\subsection{Differential gene expression of motion integrator cells using the WARP dataset}

\subsubsection{Detection of MI cells in the WARP dataset}
For 6,787 out of 16,234 segmented cells, there was a match between the RDM/split-view luminance and the WARP stimulus replication. From these cells, MIs were detected, using the regressor trace described above (\autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}A, \autoref{fig:warp brain}A). Then the WARP stimulus responses of these cells were to find potential MI neurons in the WARP dataset (\autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}C,D).\\
Out of 138,156 active cells selected from the WARP dataset (see \hyperref[sec:warp proc]{2.3.2}), 2,533 cells had a \textit{pearson} $\rho$ of at least 0.5. Curiously, most of these cells appeared on the left hemisphere of the brain, this could be due to imaging artifacts in the data.\\
Based on the regional constraints described in section \hyperref[sec:warp mi]{2.3.3}, 530 cells  of the putative MI cells located in the hindbrain were selected for differential gene expression 
analysis (\autoref{fig:warp brain}B).\\
The response traces, of the selected cells from the WARP dataset, show the same increased activity to motion in ipsilateral direction, as well as the slight activity during forward and backward motion, as the MI cells detected by RDM. But they lack the response to the response to the contralateral loom stimulus (\autoref{fig:warp mi regressors}C, \autoref{fig:warp brain}C,D). This could be caused by the recreated loom stimulus being different then the one used in the WARP dataset, and therefore evoking less of a response in motion sensing cells.\\
